# Northstar Account Review Brief Test

> Fictional example. Northstar, its team, customers and activity are invented.

Northstar's customer success team wanted a quicker way to prepare for its weekly internal account review. The team didn't ask the AI tool to score account health. It tested whether a structured first draft helped people see what was known, what was open and what still needed judgement.

## The First Test: What Actually Happened

Marcus, a customer success manager, used the method ahead of a review for Thackeray Logistics.

**Marcus's notes, pasted into the prompt:**

> Thackeray Logistics, contract renews in three months. Daily active users dropped from four to two over the last six weeks. No support tickets raised in that time. Last QBR was five months ago; the only note from it is "call went fine."

**First draft (Version 1), as the tool produced it:**

> **Account at a glance:** Thackeray Logistics, renewal in three months.
>
> **Account health: At risk.** Usage has dropped by half over six weeks and there has been no recent stakeholder contact, both signs of a weakening relationship ahead of renewal.
>
> **Open actions:** none confirmed.

**What was wrong with it:** the brief labelled the account "at risk," which the prompt rules out ("Do not label an account healthy, at risk or likely to renew"). A drop in logins and a quiet stakeholder are real signals worth checking. But the draft turned them into a conclusion nobody had reached.

## The Second Test

Four volunteers used fictional account notes in the first session, then tried the revised method with information approved for their existing tool. Each person checked the brief against their notes before discussing it with colleagues.

**Second draft (Version 2), after the revision:**

> **Account at a glance:** Thackeray Logistics, renewal in three months.
>
> **What has happened:** daily active users dropped from four to two over the last six weeks. No support tickets raised in that window.
>
> **Questions to check:** does the drop reflect reduced day-to-day need, a champion change, or something else? The last recorded QBR note ("call went fine") does not say enough to judge sentiment either way.
>
> **Internal review agenda:** 1. What do we actually know about who is still using the product day to day? 2. Is there a champion change we have not tracked? 3. What would we need to know before the renewal conversation?

I checked this draft against the revised prompt. It has no Open actions section, although the table below says the team kept one. The notes confirm no actions, so the section should still have appeared, as it did in Version 1.

| What the volunteers found | What the team changed or kept |
| --- | --- |
| The brief made actions and missing owners easier to spot | Kept a separate open actions section |
| The first version made a quiet customer sound like a risk | Changed the prompt to turn weak signals into questions to check |
| People wanted a short agenda for the review | Added three internal review questions |
| One volunteer added a renewal assumption that was not in the notes | Made the quality check explicit about renewal and account health judgement |

## Why It Earned A Place

The method helped more than one person prepare an internal discussion. The safe input, useful output and human check were clear. The team could name its most common error, with Marcus's "at risk" label as the reference case, and explain what the tool must not decide.

It became an optional shared method for preparing internal account reviews. The account owner still decided whether an account was healthy, what mattered most and what should happen next.

## What This Does Not Prove

- That the method predicts renewal or account health.
- That every account review will be faster.
- That the team should use the same method for every customer.
- That a summary can replace the account owner's judgement or customer conversation.

The evidence was modest. Several people found the structure helpful. They caught a real case of the tool overstating a weak signal, and they knew where the human decision still sat.
