# Northstar Asset Review

> Fictional example. Northstar, its team, customers and activity are invented.

Northstar first tested the [Call Follow Up Draft](README.md) method with five volunteers. The first version gave a useful structure, but it sometimes made a possible next step sound agreed. The team held off sharing it.

## The First Test: What Actually Happened

Dana, an account executive, used the method after a call with Owen Bramwell at Bramwell & Co.

**Dana's notes, pasted into the prompt:**

> Bramwell & Co, currently on Starter tier. Owen mentioned their team has grown to 12 seats; Starter caps at 10. He asked for a case study from a similar-sized company on Growth tier. He said "maybe worth looking at Growth before our renewal in Q2." No next call booked; he said he'd have a look and come back to me.

**First draft (Version 1), as the tool produced it:**

> Subject: Great speaking today, Owen
>
> Hi Owen, thanks for the call. As discussed, Bramwell & Co is ready to move to Growth ahead of your Q2 renewal, and we'll reconvene next Thursday to walk through the details. I'll also send over the case study you asked about.

**What was wrong with it:** two things. "Ready to move to Growth" turns "maybe worth looking at" into a decision Owen never made. "We'll reconvene next Thursday" invents a call nobody booked. Owen said he'd come back to Dana, not the other way round.

## The Second Test

Three volunteers, including Dana, used a revised version over two weeks. The revised prompt told the tool to flag unclear details and to label any next step nobody had agreed as a suggestion.

**Second draft (Version 2), after the revision:**

> Subject: Following up from today
>
> Hi Owen, thanks for the call. I hear Growth might be worth a look ahead of your Q2 renewal, no pressure either way. I'll send the case study you asked about so you've got something concrete to weigh up.
>
> Check Before Sending: confirm whether "maybe worth looking at" is worth restating this plainly, and there's no next call booked yet, so nothing above should read as one.

| What the volunteers found | What the team changed or kept |
| --- | --- |
| The draft removed the blank page after calls with clear notes | Kept the short, structured email format |
| The assumptions list helped people catch missing owners and dates | Kept **Check Before Sending** as a required part of the output |
| One draft still sounded too generic | Added the instruction to edit in the salesperson's own voice |
| No one wanted the method to send messages | Kept sending and final approval with the salesperson |

## Why It Earned a Place

More than one person used the method more than once. The safe input, useful output and human check were clear. The team knew its common mistakes, with Dana's case as the reference example, and chose an owner to review it after a month.

It became a shared team method, not a required process. People could adapt it when a situation needed something different.

## What This Does Not Prove

- That every follow up will be better.
- That the team saved a specific number of hours.
- That the method will work in another organisation without adapting it.
- That the AI should make a customer commitment or send an email.

The evidence was simple. Several people found it helpful and understood its limits. They could also point to a real case, Dana's Version 1 against Version 2, where the check caught something that mattered.
